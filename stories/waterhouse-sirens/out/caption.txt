These sirens aren't mermaids. They're birds with women's heads, and in 1891 that shocked London.

In Homer's Odyssey (the story in Christopher Nolan's new film), the sirens sing so sweetly that no sailor who hears them gets home. Ulysses has his crew plug their ears with wax and tie him to the mast so he alone can listen.

When John William Waterhouse showed this at the Royal Academy, viewers expected lovely, mermaid-like women. He gave them feathers and talons. The Times called some of it "grotesque" and "even ridiculous".

But Homer never describes the sirens. Greek artists painted them as birds with women's heads, and Waterhouse borrowed his from a Greek vase in the British Museum, painted around 480–470 BC. One siren on it dives into the sea. The fish tails only came in the Middle Ages.

The vase painter's name is lost. Scholars call him the Siren Painter.

P.S. Melbourne's gallery bought the painting in 1891, the year it was shown, for £1,200.
