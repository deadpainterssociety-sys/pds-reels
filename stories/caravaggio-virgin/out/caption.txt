Caravaggio painted the death of the Virgin Mary for a chapel in a Carmelite church in Rome, around 1606. No angels, no clouds of glory: just a dead woman on a bed, her body swollen, one hand hanging limp, surrounded by grieving apostles.

The friars refused it. His rival Giovanni Baglione later wrote that he had painted the Madonna swollen, with bare legs. Giulio Mancini, a physician who wrote about painters of the time, claimed the model was a prostitute (historians treat the model stories as rumour).

Then a young Peter Paul Rubens, court painter in Mantua, told the Duke Vincenzo Gonzaga it was one of Caravaggio's best works. The duke bought it, and before it left Rome it was shown for one week, 1–7 April 1607. Copying was forbidden.

The painting a chapel didn't want went on to King Charles I of England in 1627, and in 1671 to Louis XIV of France.

P.S. The friars replaced it with a much tamer Death of the Virgin by Carlo Saraceni.
