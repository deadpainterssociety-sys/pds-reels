In 1781, in London, the Swiss painter Henry Fuseli painted a woman asleep, thrown back across her bed, with a demon crouching on her chest. And a horse's head, with blank white eyes, pushing through the curtain.

But the horse isn't the nightmare. In old Germanic and Scandinavian folklore, the mare was a spirit that sat on sleepers' chests, tormenting and suffocating them. That is the 'mare' in nightmare. It has nothing to do with horses.

Fuseli's first drawing, from March 1781, had no horse at all: his friend and biographer John Knowles wrote that the head was added later, in the painting. Scholars read it as a visual pun.

Shown at the Royal Academy in 1782, it caused a sensation. Fuseli sold it for twenty guineas; the publisher of the prints said he made over five hundred pounds.
