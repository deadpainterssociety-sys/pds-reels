This smile shocked Paris.

In 1786 Élisabeth Vigée Le Brun, Marie Antoinette's portrait painter, painted herself hugging her daughter Julie, and she showed it at the Paris Salon of 1787. Look closely: her lips are parted, and you can see her teeth.

That was the problem. As the historian Colin Jones explains, in Western art an open mouth had long belonged to the poor, the mad or small children, the people who couldn't control themselves, like Frans Hals's laughing Malle Babbe. And in an age of sugar, real teeth were often bad. Elegant sitters kept their lips closed.

One commentator was outraged. Smiling so that she shows her teeth, he wrote, was "an affectation which artists, art-lovers and persons of taste have been united in condemning", and "particularly out of place in a mother".
