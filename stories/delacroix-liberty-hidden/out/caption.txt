In July 1830, Paris rose up. Three days of street fighting drove out King Charles X. Eugène Delacroix didn't fight, but he wrote to his brother: "if I haven't fought for my country at least I'll paint for her." He painted Liberty herself, leading the people over the dead.

The revolution crowned a new king, Louis-Philippe. In 1831 his government bought the painting and showed it at the Luxembourg Palace. Within months it came down: its message was too inflammatory. After Paris rose again in June 1832, it was handed back to Delacroix. In 1848 the critic Champfleury wrote that it had been "hidden in an attic for being too revolutionary."

A king crowned by a revolution couldn't risk a painting of one. It reached the Louvre only in 1874, under the Third Republic.

P.S. The 2023–24 restoration removed eight layers of varnish and revealed that Liberty's dress was once grey, not yellow.
